    How the needles work

    Each needle shows one estimate: the chance the candidate on that side wins. It is this site's own rough model, not a professional forecast.

    • Senate control (the needle at the top) adds up the ten needles and the 25 other seats up this year, allowing for polling misses that run the same way everywhere. Republicans keep control with 50 seats, because the vice president breaks ties; Democrats need 51. If Dan Osborn, an independent who has said he won't caucus with either party, wins Nebraska and the Senate splits 50 to 49, he holds the balance. Seats not tracked here use Decision Desk HQ's posted chances where it gave them and are otherwise treated as safe or likely. Kalshi, Polymarket and Decision Desk HQ are shown beside it for comparison; market prices are not part of it.
    • Before votes are counted the needle sits on its starting point (below), allowing about 5 to 6 points of polling error.
    • As counties report, each county's count is compared with how it voted for president in 2024. The average shift in counties that are mostly counted is applied to the votes still to come everywhere. Partly counted counties count for less, because early and mail ballots, which often lean one way, are usually counted first.
    • Results elsewhere count too. Polling misses tend to run the same way across the country, so part of any state's surprise is treated as shared. When a state's official count runs ahead of or behind its starting point (say, Democrats doing much worse than expected in Florida), every other race's starting point moves by the shared part. The move is bigger the more of that state's vote is in, and each card says how far it moved. A state's own count still outweighs this as its votes come in. Only official counts feed this, never market prices.
    • Early clues. Races that report early work the same way, though they aren't shown here: five House races from Axios's list (Florida's 14th and 25th, Virginia's 1st, 2nd and 5th), Indiana's 1st District, and Kentucky's Senate race. Each has an expected result: its 2024 presidential margin, plus the forecast national swing, plus about 2 points for an incumbent. When a count runs ahead of or behind that, the shared part moves every Senate needle. Districts count for less than a statewide race, and only once a few precincts are in.
    • The range under each needle is the middle 80% of likely final margins. It narrows as the share of the expected vote still out shrinks.
    • Market ticks (K for Kalshi, P for Polymarket) are betting prices, shown separately. They are not part of the estimate. The hollow mark is the starting point before the count.
    • Race calls appear only when the AP's, Decision Desk HQ's or Dave Wasserman's ("I've seen enough") own post says so, with a link. The needle keeps showing the model.
    • G. Elliott Morris's and Decision Desk HQ's forecasts are built in. Each needle's starting point blends Morris's final forecast (50%), Decision Desk HQ's last posted forecast (30%) and the final polling average (20%). When Morris posts a win probability for a race on Bluesky tonight, it is blended into that needle at up to half weight, fading by half each hour and as the official count comes in.
    • Where the counts come from. Ohio, Florida, Alaska and Nebraska are read straight from their official election-night files: county by county, except Nebraska, which gives statewide totals only. Texas, Michigan, South Carolina, Iowa and Kansas block automated readers, and Maine posts no live count. For those states the count is Decision Desk HQ's latest posted update on Bluesky (statewide totals and its estimate of the share counted), and the card says so. Decision Desk HQ's website itself is not read; its terms forbid that. Until such an update appears after polls close, the needle shows the Kalshi and Polymarket average, labelled "market odds".
    • Alaska counts absentee ballots for two weeks and uses ranked choice, so its range stays wide.
